# The 1000 m² Mars Rubber-Glass House — Version 2.0

## Abstract

This paper presents the Version 2.0 design of the 1000 m² Mars Rubber-Glass House, a comprehensive upgrade from the structural feasibility study (v1, June 2024) into a fully specified habitat for long-duration human habitation. Building on the Design Improvements v2 proposals, v2.0 integrates five major new systems: closed-loop waste-to-resource cycling, structured crew routines informed by Earth analogue experience, in-situ resource utilisation (ISRU), a medical and psychological support framework, and a simulation and validation methodology. The result transforms the Rubber-Glass House from a shelter into a self-sustaining micro-civilisation capable of supporting four crew members indefinitely.

---

## 1. Introduction

### 1.1 Background

The original Mars Rubber-Glass House (v1) established the structural and material foundation: a 1000 m² (50 m × 20 m) single-storey habitat with silicone rubber foundations, a titanium/Inconel frame, borosilicate glass walls, rooftop solar panels, and a single airlock — all at a materials cost of approximately $1.02 M ([DOI: 10.5281/zenodo.16493055](https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.16493055)).

The Design Improvements v2 document identified critical gaps in the v1 design and proposed eight upgrades:

1. Interior height increase from 3 m to 5 m
2. Three thermal zones (warm, temperate, cool)
3. Closed-loop water management
4. Multi-layered radiation shielding
5. Agricultural system expansion with vertical farming
6. Airlock redundancy (2 + emergency hatch)
7. Energy storage and redundancy (100 kWh battery + RHU)
8. Acoustics and crew privacy (individual cabins)

### 1.2 Scope of Version 2.0

Version 2.0 accepts all eight v2 proposals as baseline and extends the design into a **complete operational habitat** by addressing five previously unspecified domains:

- **Closed-loop waste-to-resource cycling** — from human excreta and food waste to fertiliser, water, and chemical feedstocks
- **Structured crew routines** — daily, weekly, monthly, and annual schedules informed by Biosphere 2, MARS-500, HI-SEAS, ISS, and Antarctic station operations
- **In-situ resource utilisation (ISRU)** — manufacturing construction materials, propellants, and speciality chemicals from Martian resources
- **Medical and psychological support** — informed by confined-environment psychology research
- **Simulation and validation methodology** — a rigorous cycle of Investigation → Model → Simulation → Validation → Scientific Assessment

### 1.3 Lessons from Earth Isolation Analogues

The v2.0 design is informed by operational experience from five key analogues:

| Analogue | Duration | Crew | Key Lessons |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Biosphere 2** (1991–1993) | 2 years | 8 | Agriculture produced 83% of diet; O₂ dropped from 20.9% to 14.5%; CO₂ management critical; crew lost 16% body weight; crew factions formed; constructed wetlands recycled sewage |
| **MARS-500** (2010–2011) | 520 days | 6 | Sleep disruption in 4/6 crew; activity levels collapsed; circadian rhythm disruption; no interpersonal conflicts reported officially |
| **HI-SEAS IV** (2015–2016) | 366 days | 6 | Food morale critical; water system failures occurred; communication delay simulation; crew dynamics under study |
| **Concordia Station** (ongoing) | 9 months/winter | 13 | Extreme isolation; hypobaric hypoxia at 3,233 m; circadian disruption; analogue for deep-space missions |
| **ISS** (ongoing) | 6–12 months | 3–6 | Noise and privacy are top stressors; structured schedules essential; exercise prevents bone/muscle loss; WRS achieves 93% water recovery |

These lessons directly inform every section of this document.

## 3. Closed-Loop Life Support System (CLLSS)

The v2.0 habitat operates as a near-closed bioregenerative life-support system. The goal is **≥ 95% closure** for water and **≥ 80% closure** for food, following the Biosphere 2 model but improved with 30 years of subsequent research.

### 3.1 Atmospheric Management

| Parameter | Target | Method |
|---|---|---|
| O₂ partial pressure | 21.0 kPa (± 0.5) | Photosynthesis + electrolysis backup |
| CO₂ partial pressure | 0.04–0.10 kPa | Plant uptake + scrubber fallback |
| Total pressure | 70 kPa | Pressure vessel + relief valves |
| Relative humidity | 40–60% | Condensation + dehumidification |
| Temperature (warm zone) | 22–24 °C | Active heating + thermal mass |
| Trace gas removal | < SMAC limits | Activated carbon + catalytic oxidiser |

**Lesson from Biosphere 2:** O₂ declined from 20.9% to 14.5% over 16 months due to microbial respiration in organic-rich soils and CO₂ absorption by unsealed concrete. V2.0 mitigates this through:
- Sealed silicone rubber floor preventing soil–concrete reactions
- Dedicated algae bioreactors (40 m²) optimised for O₂ production
- Backup electrolysis unit (splitting water → H₂ + O₂)
- Real-time atmospheric monitoring with automated adjustment

### 3.2 Water Management

**Water budget per crew member (with recycling):**

| Source | Volume/day | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Drinking water | 2.5 L | Potable grade |
| Food preparation | 1.0 L | |
| Personal hygiene | 6.0 L | Shower (Navy-style, 2 min) |
| Laundry | 5.0 L | Weekly, shared |
| Agriculture irrigation | 8.0 L | Recirculating hydroponic |
| Toilet flush / processing | 2.5 L | Vacuum-assist |
| **Total per person** | **25 L/day** | |
| **Total for 4 crew** | **100 L/day** | |

**Water recycling system (modelled on ISS WRS):**

1. **Humidity condensate recovery** — Air handlers collect ~4 L/day per person from transpiration and respiration
2. **Greywater filtration** — Shower, laundry, and kitchen water filtered through biofilm reactor + activated carbon + UV sterilisation
3. **Urine processing** — Vapour compression distillation (VCD) achieving 93% recovery rate
4. **Plant transpiration recovery** — The agricultural zone alone recycles ~60 L/day through plant transpiration → condensation
5. **Underfloor reservoir** — 10 m³ emergency reserve integrated into silicone rubber floor slab (inherently watertight)

**Target water recovery rate:** ≥ 93% (ISS demonstrated), aspiration ≥ 97% with plant transpiration loop.

### 3.3 Waste-to-Resource Cycling

This is a **key innovation of v2.0**. Every waste stream is treated as a resource input:

#### 3.3.1 Human Excreta Processing

| Waste Stream | Daily Volume (4 crew) | Processing | Output |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Urine** | ~6 L | VCD → water; urea extraction → nitrogen fertiliser | ~5.5 L water + ~30 g urea/day |
| **Faeces** | ~0.6 kg | Thermophilic composting (60–70 °C, 14 days) → vermicomposting (21 days) | ~0.15 kg stabilised compost/day |
| **Greywater sludge** | ~2 L | Biofilm digestion → nutrient extraction | Phosphorus + potassium salts |

**Composting process:**
1. **Collection** — Vacuum toilet separates urine and faeces at source
2. **Pre-treatment** — Faeces mixed with crop residue (C:N ratio 25:1–30:1)
3. **Thermophilic composting** — Enclosed reactor at 60–70 °C for 14 days; kills pathogens (Salmonella, E. coli, helminth ova)
4. **Vermicomposting** — *Eisenia fetida* (red wiggler worms) process compost for additional 21 days, producing nutrient-rich vermicast
5. **Maturation** — 30 days aerobic curing
6. **Application** — Distributed to agricultural beds as slow-release fertiliser

**Safety:** The thermophilic stage exceeds WHO guidelines for pathogen destruction (≥ 55 °C for ≥ 14 days). Compost is tested for faecal coliforms before agricultural use.

#### 3.3.2 Food Waste Processing

| Waste Type | Daily Volume (4 crew) | Processing | Output |
|---|---|---|---|
| Crop residue (stems, roots, leaves) | ~3 kg | Shredding → composting or biochar | Compost or soil amendment |
| Kitchen waste (peels, scraps) | ~0.5 kg | Vermicomposting or anaerobic digestion | Vermicast or biogas (CH₄) |
| Inedible biomass | ~1 kg | Pyrolysis (400 °C, O₂-free) | Biochar (soil carbon) + syngas |

#### 3.3.3 Atmospheric Waste Cycling

| Gas | Source | Processing | Output |
|---|---|---|---|
| CO₂ (exhaled) | ~1 kg/person/day | Plant photosynthesis + Sabatier reactor | O₂ + biomass; or CH₄ + H₂O |
| CH₄ (from digestion) | Trace | Catalytic oxidation | CO₂ + H₂O (fed back to plants) |
| NH₃ (from composting) | Trace | Biofilter (nitrifying bacteria) | NO₃⁻ (liquid fertiliser) |
| VOCs (from materials) | Trace | Activated carbon adsorption | Cleaned air |

#### 3.3.4 Chemical and Raw Material Recovery

The closed-loop system also produces speciality outputs:

| Product | Source | Process | Use |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Nitrogen fertiliser (urea)** | Urine | Concentration + crystallisation | Agriculture |
| **Phosphorus salts** | Greywater + compost leachate | Struvite precipitation (MgNH₄PO₄) | Agriculture |
| **Potassium salts** | Plant ash + compost leachate | Leaching + evaporation | Agriculture |
| **Biochar** | Crop residue pyrolysis | Pyrolysis at 400 °C | Soil amendment, water filtration |
| **Methane (CH₄)** | Anaerobic digestion of food waste | Mesophilic digestion (35 °C) | Fuel for heating or Sabatier input |
| **Oxygen (O₂)** | Water electrolysis backup | PEM electrolysis | Atmosphere supplement |
| **Hydrogen (H₂)** | Water electrolysis | PEM electrolysis | Sabatier reactor input |

### 4.3 Aquaponics Integration

A closed-loop fish–plant system provides protein diversification and nutrient cycling:

```
┌─────────────┐     ┌──────────────┐     ┌──────────────┐
│  Fish Tanks  │────▶│ Biofilter    │────▶│ Grow Beds    │
│  (Tilapia)   │     │ (NH₃→NO₃⁻)  │     │ (Lettuce,    │
│  30 m²       │     │              │     │  tomatoes)   │
│  28°C        │     └──────────────┘     │              │
│              │◀────────────────────────│  Return water │
└─────────────┘                          └──────────────┘
     │                                         │
     ▼                                         ▼
  Fish waste                              Clean water
  (fertiliser)                            (back to tanks)
```

- **Fish species:** Tilapia (*Oreochromis niloticus*) — hardy, fast-growing, omnivorous, previously used in Biosphere 2's rice–azolla pond system
- **Feed:** Spirulina + crop residue + insect meal (black soldier fly larvae fed on food waste)
- **Yield:** ~120 kg fish/year (30 g protein/day for 4 crew)

### 4.4 Lighting System

Mars receives only ~43% of Earth's solar irradiance (590 W/m² peak at Mars vs. 1,361 W/m²). During global dust storms (lasting 2–8 weeks), solar input can drop to 10–20% of nominal.

**Supplemental LED array:**
- Full-spectrum LEDs (red 660 nm + blue 450 nm + white) — optimised for photosynthetically active radiation (PAR)
- Power requirement: ~50 W/m² × 1,200 m² effective area × 18 h/day = **1,080 kWh/day** at full power
- During clear conditions: LEDs supplement natural sunlight (reduce to ~30% power)
- During dust storms: LEDs operate at 100% to maintain crop growth
- Light scheduling: 18 h light / 6 h dark cycle (optimised for most crop species)

## 6. Radiation Shielding

### 6.1 Radiation Environment

Mars surface radiation (MSL/RAD data):
- GCR dose rate: ~0.67 mSv/day (~244 mSv/year)
- SPE events: up to 50 mSv in hours (rare but dangerous)
- NASA career limit: 600 mSv (age/sex dependent)

### 6.2 Multi-Layer Shielding Design

| Layer | Location | Material | Thickness | Dose Reduction |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Regolith blanket** | Roof (above solar panels) | Martian regolith | 50 cm (~80 g/cm²) | ~50% GCR |
| **Water walls** | Side walls (double-glass) | Water | 10–20 cm | ~20–30% GCR + excellent neutron shielding |
| **Hydrogen-rich polyethylene** | Storm shelter walls | HDPE panels | 10 cm | Additional ~15% for shelter |
| **Regolith berm** | Storm shelter | Compacted regolith | ≥ 2 m overhead | ~90% total for shelter |

### 6.3 Storm Shelter

- **Location:** Central portion of temperate zone
- **Area:** ~20 m² (5 m × 4 m)
- **Capacity:** 4 crew for up to 72 hours
- **Shielding:** ≥ 2 m regolith overhead + water walls on all sides
- **Equipment:** Emergency life support, 200 L water, food rations (72 h), medical kit, communications terminal
- **Effective dose rate in shelter:** < 0.05 mSv/day

### 6.4 Combined Shielding Performance

| Location | Dose Rate (mSv/day) | Annual Dose (mSv) |
|---|---|---|
| Unshielded Mars surface | 0.67 | 244 |
| Inside habitat (general) | ~0.20–0.25 | ~73–91 |
| Inside storm shelter | < 0.05 | < 18 |
| NASA career limit reference | — | 600 |

A 2-year stay at ~0.22 mSv/day = ~160 mSv — well within NASA career limits.

## 7. Crew Routines — Operational Schedules

### 7.1 Design Philosophy

Structured schedules are essential for crew health, morale, and productivity. Research from all major analogues confirms:

- **ISS:** Highly structured 16-hour work / 8-hour sleep schedule prevents drift
- **MARS-500:** Unstructured time led to crew becoming progressively inactive; 4 of 6 developed sleep disorders
- **Biosphere 2:** Crew faction formation was exacerbated by disagreements over daily priorities
- **HI-SEAS:** Crew performed 40 tasks/week; structured activities maintained engagement
- **Concordia:** Circadian disruption during polar night requires strict light/dark scheduling

### 7.2 Daily Schedule (Sol = 24 h 37 min)

The daily schedule follows a **16/8 rhythm** (16 h active, 8 h sleep), with solar time adjusted for Mars's slightly longer day:

| Time (Mars Local) | Duration | Activity | Zone |
|---|---|---|---|
| 06:00–06:30 | 30 min | Wake-up, personal hygiene | Warm |
| 06:30–07:00 | 30 min | Morning medical check (weight, vitals, mood log) | Warm |
| 07:00–07:45 | 45 min | Breakfast (communal) | Warm |
| 07:45–08:00 | 15 min | Daily briefing + task assignment | Temperate |
| 08:00–10:00 | 2 h | **Agricultural maintenance** — harvest, planting, pruning, nutrient checks | Cool |
| 10:00–10:15 | 15 min | Break | — |
| 10:15–12:15 | 2 h | **Science / Laboratory work** — experiments, data analysis, Earth comms | Temperate |
| 12:15–13:00 | 45 min | Lunch (communal) | Warm |
| 13:00–13:30 | 30 min | **Siesta / personal time** | Warm |
| 13:30–15:30 | 2 h | **Maintenance + Engineering** — systems checks, repairs, ISRU | Temperate |
| 15:30–15:45 | 15 min | Break | — |
| 15:45–17:00 | 1.25 h | **EVA or training** (2 crew, alternating pairs) or indoor exercise | Various |
| 17:00–18:00 | 1 h | **Mandatory exercise** — resistance training + cardio | Warm |
| 18:00–18:30 | 30 min | Personal hygiene + shower | Warm |
| 18:30–19:15 | 45 min | Dinner (communal) | Warm |
| 19:15–20:00 | 45 min | **Evening briefing** — log entries, next-sol planning, Earth video uplink | Temperate |
| 20:00–22:00 | 2 h | **Personal time** — recreation, reading, video calls, hobbies | Warm / Cabins |
| 22:00–06:00 | 8 h | **Sleep** (individual cabins, blue-light-free) | Cabins |

**Critical design features:**
- **Three communal meals** — Biosphere 2 and HI-SEAS both found shared meals are the most important social bonding activity
- **Mandatory exercise** — ISS protocol: 2 h/day (1 h resistance + 1 h cardio) prevents bone loss (~1.5%/month) and muscle atrophy
- **Morning medical check** — Body weight, blood pressure, pulse oximetry, mood self-assessment on a 1–10 scale (MARS-500 found early detection of psychological issues is critical)
- **Personal time** — Minimum 2 h/day unscheduled time (ISS finding: overscheduling causes crew rebellion — see Skylab 4 "strike")
- **Blue-light curfew** — No blue-spectrum lighting after 20:00 (Concordia research shows circadian disruption is a major issue)

### 7.3 Weekly Schedule

Each sol has a primary focus. The week is structured around a **6-day work / 1-day rest** cycle (consistent with ISS operations):

| Sol | Focus | Special Activities |
|---|---|---|
| **Sol 1 (Monday)** | Agriculture | Deep harvest; crop rotation planning; seed germination checks; compost turning |
| **Sol 2 (Tuesday)** | Science | Laboratory experiments; geological sample analysis; Earth data downlink |
| **Sol 3 (Wednesday)** | Engineering | Systems maintenance; filter changes; pressure checks; ISRU operations |
| **Sol 4 (Thursday)** | EVA Day | Pair EVA (2 crew, 4 h); regolith collection; exterior inspection; sensor deployment |
| **Sol 5 (Friday)** | Medical + Training | Full medical examinations; emergency drills; skills cross-training |
| **Sol 6 (Saturday)** | Housekeeping | Deep cleaning; laundry; waste processing check; inventory review |

       │                                     │

**Applied to each domain:**

## 8. In-Situ Resource Utilisation (ISRU)

### 8.1 Available Martian Resources

| Resource | Source | Abundance | Processing |
|---|---|---|---|
| **SiO₂ (silicon dioxide)** | Regolith (~45% SiO₂) | Abundant | Glass, silicone rubber, aerogel |
| **Fe₂O₃ (iron oxide)** | Regolith (~18% Fe₂O₃) | Abundant | Iron/steel for tools, structures |
| **CO₂** | Atmosphere (95.3%) | Effectively unlimited | Sabatier → CH₄ + O₂; feedstock for plant growth |
| **N₂** | Atmosphere (2.7%) | Limited but extractable | Fertiliser (Haber–Bosch requires H₂ + N₂) |
| **H₂O (ice)** | Subsurface ice deposits | Variable by location | Water, H₂ (electrolysis), O₂ |
| **Ar (argon)** | Atmosphere (1.6%) | Available | Inert gas for welding, fire suppression |
| **Al₂O₃, TiO₂, MgO** | Regolith | Minor components | Aluminium, titanium extraction (long-term) |

### 8.2 ISRU Processing Chain

**Phase 1 (from Sol 1): Atmospheric Processing**
- CO₂ collection + Sabatier reaction: CO₂ + 4H₂ → CH₄ + 2H₂O
- Produces methane (fuel/heating) + water
- Water electrolysis: 2H₂O → 2H₂ + O₂
- Oxygen for atmosphere; hydrogen recycled to Sabatier

**Phase 2 (from Sol 100): Regolith Processing**
- Regolith collection by robotic systems ([DOI: 10.5281/zenodo.15455156](https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.15455156))
- Silica extraction for glass repair and silicone rubber production
- Iron oxide reduction for basic metal tools
- Regolith sintering for construction bricks

**Phase 3 (from Sol 300): Chemical Manufacturing**
- Haber–Bosch process (N₂ + H₂ → NH₃) — requires significant energy but enables fertiliser independence
- Perchlorate reduction from regolith — detoxification of Martian soil for agriculture
- Water extraction from subsurface ice deposits

## 15. Conclusion

Version 2.0 of the Mars Rubber-Glass House transforms the original concept from a structural feasibility study into a comprehensive design for indefinite human habitation on Mars. The key advances are:

1. **From shelter to ecosystem** — The closed-loop life support system with waste-to-resource cycling creates a micro-biosphere where human waste feeds crops that feed humans, approaching the ideal of a self-sustaining system.

2. **From blueprint to operations manual** — Structured daily, weekly, monthly, and annual routines, informed by decades of analogue experience, address the human factors that make or break isolated communities.

3. **From Earth-dependent to Mars-native** — The ISRU strategy progressively reduces dependence on Earth resupply, consistent with the Three-Step Evolution roadmap toward autonomous structures ([DOI: 10.5281/zenodo.16540444](https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.16540444)).

4. **From theoretical to validated** — The five-stage simulation methodology ensures every system is continuously investigated, modelled, simulated, validated, and scientifically assessed.

The approximately $3 M materials cost (or ~$108 M including transport) positions the Mars Rubber-Glass House as a serious candidate for early Martian settlement. As ISRU capabilities mature and transport costs decline — SpaceX targets $200/kg to LEO within the decade — the economics become increasingly favourable.

> *"A house is not a home until it is lived in. On Mars, a house is not a home until it can sustain the lives within it — indefinitely, gracefully, and with dignity."*
